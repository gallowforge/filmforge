# The story engine in depth

## Why six engines

Short films have no time to explain. Every engine is a way to make the audience feel something with a picture
instead of a sentence. A film with all six lands even without words. The examples are from The Lantern Moth
(`examples/the-lantern-moth/story-bible.md`), the repository's worked example.

### 1. One relationship

Pick two. The film is about what changes between them. In The Lantern Moth it is a lighthouse girl and the ember
moth, the first warm thing she has held all winter. Side characters, and the world itself, exist to put pressure on
the pair: the boat drifting toward the rocks is the pressure.

### 2. One flaw, with a price

The flaw must cost someone else, on screen. "She is lonely" is a trait; "she wants to keep the warmth for herself,
and while she cups it the boat drifts toward the rocks" is a flaw with a price. The price has to be in a frame the
audience can see (the red sail almost on the rocks, through the salt-crusted glass).

### 3. One choice that breaks your heart

The hero gives up what they wanted, by their own hand, at the moment it costs most. The girl opens her hands toward
the dead lamp and lets the moth go into it. The choice must be a visible action, because the audience only sees
actions, and it belongs on the music's biggest change (here, the held breath before the hit).

### 4. One twist

The truth was in plain sight. The moth does not burn out: at dawn the lamp glass is covered in a hundred small ember
moths. Plant every piece of the twist earlier: the moth on her fingertip in s03 is the seed of the hundred in s06,
and the lamp it goes into is the frame the film opened on.

### 5. One mirror

An image that lies, then an image that tells the truth. Her reflection in the lamp lens: alone and lit by the moth
in s03, then lost in the beam's light in s05. The first says the warmth is hers; the second says it was never only
hers.

### 6. One image, twice

The same composition opens and closes the film, changed by everything between. The dead lighthouse on its black
headland at night (s01), then the same frame at dawn, the lamp warm and the boat safe (s06). It is also the cheapest
engine: one plate serves both shots.

## Setups and payoffs

Build the table before the screenplay. Every motif that matters appears at least twice: the empty lantern she
carries everywhere stays behind on the floor once the moth is gone; one moth on her fingertip becomes a hundred on
the glass; the film's refusal of warm gold pays off when the beam blazes gold on the hit. A prop that appears once
is either cut or given its return.

## Writing to music that exists

When the music comes first (an original piece, a song), its sections are the scenes. Write one mood line per
section and one or two images per mood. Pages or shots turn on its phrases; the story's turn sits on its biggest
change; the end sits on its final chord.

## Words

- Wordless is strongest when the six engines are in place.
- When there are words, each line says what no picture can: who is speaking and why it matters, a name, a stake, a
  meaning. Never describe the picture on screen. The Lantern Moth's last line, "some lights are only kept by giving
  them away.", says what the ending means and nothing the frame already shows.
- One voice, consistent. A voice that looks back from later can say what no one in the film knows yet.

## Two worlds, two hands

When the story has two worlds (grief and life, winter and spring), the drawing itself can carry it: a hard, ruled
line and a narrow grey palette for the frozen world; round, flowing lines and full colour for the living one. As the
film goes on, the worlds leak into each other. That decision belongs in the bible; forge-style-lock writes the two
looks.
